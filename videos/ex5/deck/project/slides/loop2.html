Step two: the centres. The centre of the old points and the centre of their partners are just averages: add everything up, then divide by the count. static_cast turns the count into a decimal number; in C++ it’s the safe, explicit way to convert a type. Once the rotation is known, the shift is just the difference of the two centres. Step three: the matrix H. For every pair it takes the old point minus its centre, times the new point minus its centre turned into a row. Subtracting the centres removes the shift, so what’s left in H is only how the two shapes are turned against each other. Step four: the singular value decomposition splits H into U, sigma and V. This is the Kabsch method: it gives the best rotation directly, R equals V times U transposed, with no searching. One check: if R’s determinant is negative, R is a mirror image, not a rotation; flipping the last column of V gives the best real rotation. Then the shift is the new centre minus the rotated old centre. Testat question: where does R = V·Uᵀ come from? We want the rotation that makes the summed squared gaps smallest. With centred points that’s the same as maximising the trace of R times H, and the SVD gives that maximum at V Uᵀ.
